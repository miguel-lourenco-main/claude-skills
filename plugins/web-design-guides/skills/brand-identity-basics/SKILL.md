# Brand identity basics

A site inherits its brand. A logo or colour picked for looks alone breaks at favicon size, on a
dark header, or in the wrong market. This skill is an **index**: §1–6 settle most decisions, and
§7 points to one distilled note per guide in `references/` beside this file.

## 1 · Set the brand system before any mark

- A brand has four parts that must agree: mission/values, visual identity, voice and positioning.
  Visuals without the other three are decoration.
- Positioning: "For [target], [brand] is the [category] that [benefit] because [evidence]". The
  "because" makes it defensible.
- Guidelines fit on one page with concrete values (hex/CMYK, fonts, voice samples, placement).
  Consistency across every touchpoint builds trust faster than one-off polish.

## 2 · Choose the logo type first

Type decides how the mark behaves everywhere: favicon, embroidery, signage.

- **Taxonomy**: wordmark, lettermark/monogram, emblem, mascot, abstract mark, combination mark.
- **New brands** launch a combination mark (icon + name). Go icon-only after recognition is
  earned and the icon passes the smallest-size test.
- **Mascots and emblems** last through an authentic origin story and consistency, not redesigns.
- **Style** matches the brand's tier and positioning. Barbershop, church and fashion identities
  are the same decision applied to a vertical.
- **Trends**: minimalist wordmarks in luxury fashion went from boom to backlash within a few years.
  Don't copy a trend as if it were timeless.

## 3 · Select type for the mark

- A logo font must pass three checks: legible at every size, carries the intended personality,
  and is licensed for commercial use.
- Serif reads as tradition, authority or premium; sans-serif as modern or accessible; bold display
  as energy or confidence.
- Test at four sizes, from 8 pt favicon to 72 pt+ signage. The brand uses 2–3 typefaces at most.

## 4 · Choose colour as strategy

- Match the psychological effect to what positioning needs: urgency/appetite vs trust/calm. The
  effect that helps one category hurts another.
- Shade carries meaning, not just hue: bright, deep and muted versions of one hue position
  differently.
- A colour competitors don't use buys distinctiveness. Validate cultural meaning in the actual
  target market.
- **Pairings**: base + white is clean and accessible; + black is premium or dramatic; + gold/metal
  is luxury; + a blue/green-family colour balances energy with reliability.
- Keep the palette to 2–3 strategic colours.

## 5 · Hold the production and accessibility floor

- Contrast ≥4.5:1 for a reversed/white mark against its ground, and for brand colour used as text
  on white.
- The mark must work in pure black-and-white. If it fails in grayscale, the form is the problem,
  not the colour.
- **Simplify for single-colour and small use**: 1–2 shapes, and remove detail smaller than ~10% of
  the logo's height. Shrink-test at a one-inch square and at 16–29 px icon size.
- Audit negative space in reversed marks (squint, or view at 50% opacity).
- Check colour-blind impact; red–green affects ~8% of men.
- Export transparency-dependent marks as SVG or PNG-24, never JPG, and confirm the transparency
  in the preview.
- Deliver the full variant set: full colour, black, white/reversed, single colour, favicon/icon.

## 6 · Test with the audience, not the team

- Ask 5–10 real target users specific questions ("does this feel [adjective]?", "what do you
  recall after 5 seconds?") and look for patterns, not single opinions.
- For trust-critical categories such as healthcare or finance, test trust and appropriateness per
  stakeholder group, not preference.
- Borrowed or subversive symbolism only suits brands that openly trade on transgression, and it
  carries legal risk. Everywhere else it is a liability.
