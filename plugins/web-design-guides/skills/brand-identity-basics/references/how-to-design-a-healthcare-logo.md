## Use when
Designing a mark for a digital-first, trust-critical product (healthcare, but the platform-icon and accessibility rules apply to any app-icon-first brand — fintech, legal, safety-critical tools).

## Rules
- Design for the smallest real deployment context first, not the desktop mockup: iOS requires legibility at 29x29px within a ~20% corner-radius safe zone (from a 1024x1024px master asset, no transparency); Android adaptive icons require critical elements inside a 66dp safe circle within a 108dp canvas, since the mask shape (circle/squircle/rounded-square/teardrop) varies by device. If the mark survives the smallest icon size, it works everywhere else.
- Avoid text or fine detail in an app icon — both platforms' own guidance explicitly warns against it: text becomes illegible and reads as clutter at reduced sizes.
- Legacy category symbols can carry real liability, not just staleness: a historically-inaccurate medical symbol looks dated, and a red-cross-style mark specifically risks trademark/legal restriction for commercial use. Check for this before adopting a "standard" symbol from an old category.
- The category has moved from institutional symbols (crosses, caduceus, stethoscopes) toward abstract geometric or nature-inspired motifs signaling partnership/innovation/connectivity — human-centered imagery (hands, circles-of-care) and negative space with dual meaning are current alternatives; matching the shift signals contemporary positioning over institutional authority.
- Color psychology in trust-critical categories is evidence-backed, not just convention: cool colors (blue/green) are associated with calm/trust/reduced stress; warm colors can be used for engagement in consumer-facing sub-segments but should be used cautiously to avoid raising anxiety in vulnerable users.
- Typography for trust-critical digital products should be sans-serif for screen-rendering/accessibility reasons, minimum 16px body text, 1.2-1.6 line height; rounded letterforms read as friendlier/safer, sharp angles carry subconscious threat associations worth avoiding for anxious-user contexts.
- Meet WCAG 2.1 minimums even though the logo itself is technically exempt: 4.5:1 contrast for text, 3:1 for UI components — the brand name and adjacent interface elements must comply since real users include elderly and visually-impaired populations at above-average rates in this category.
- Test trust, not just aesthetic preference: run five-second tests (what do people remember/feel after 5 seconds), A/B preference tests across 2-3 variants measuring perceived trustworthiness specifically, and separate qualitative sessions with each real stakeholder group (patients, providers, administrators) since each will surface different concerns.

## Patterns (name — when to use — when to avoid)
- Abstract geometric or human-centered mark (hands, circles, connectivity motifs) — modern digital health/trust-critical products — avoid if the audience specifically expects/trusts traditional institutional iconography (e.g. some clinical/legacy contexts).
- Icon-only mark with no wordmark — only after testing legibility at the smallest platform icon size (29x29 iOS) — avoid if any text or fine linework disappears at that size; simplify or fall back to a bolder abstract shape instead.

## Anti-patterns
- Designing only for a desktop/large mockup and never testing at 16x16 or 29x29px.
- Reusing a red-cross-style symbol commercially without checking trademark restrictions.
- Sharp-angled, high-contrast aesthetics in a context serving anxious/vulnerable users.
- Treating logo testing as pure aesthetic preference instead of measuring trust/appropriateness directly.

## Checklist (only if supported)
- Mark tested and legible at 29x29px (iOS) and within Android's 66dp safe circle.
- No text or fine detail relied upon at icon scale.
- Any traditional/legacy symbol checked for trademark risk.
- Color choice matches evidence-based psychology for the specific sub-audience (calm vs. engagement).
- Trust perception tested directly (five-second test, stakeholder-specific qualitative sessions), not just visual preference.
