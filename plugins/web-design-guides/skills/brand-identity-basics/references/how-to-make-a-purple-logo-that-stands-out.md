## Use when
Deciding whether purple (or evaluating any low-adoption color) fits a brand, picking a specific shade, and choosing a supporting palette.

## Rules
- Statistical rarity is itself a strategic argument: a color with low adoption among competitors in your category creates automatic visual distinction — check category-color saturation (e.g. what % of comparable brands already use blue/red) before picking a color purely on preference.
- Shade selection should map to a specific emotional register, not "purple" as one undifferentiated choice: lighter/desaturated (lavender-type) = gentleness/approachability, for wellness/personal-care; bright/saturated (electric-type) = energy/modernity, for tech/digital-first (test specifically: does it pop on a phone screen amid competing content); very dark/desaturated (deep-violet-type) = luxury/authority, for premium/high-trust positioning; a warm-shifted (magenta-leaning) variant = bold/expressive, for entertainment/youth-oriented brands.
- Color pairings carry their own connotations independent of the base color: base+metallic-gold = luxury; base+white = clean/minimal/approachable; base+a natural/green accent = eco-conscious innovation; base+its complementary hue = high-energy contrast/attention; base+black = premium authority without warmth. Choose the pairing to match positioning, not just what "looks nice" together.
- Avoid pairing a busy/complex base color with additional bright colors or muddy neutrals — added visual complexity dilutes rather than reinforces the message; also avoid low-contrast pairings (e.g. a saturated color against bright yellow) that create readability strain.
- The foundational usability test for any color-dependent mark: does it still work in pure black and white? If the design relies entirely on color to read, it fails in any single-color application (fax, embroidery, engraving, grayscale print).
- White space requirements scale with color intensity: a visually intense/saturated base color needs more surrounding white space to avoid overwhelming a composition — crowded layouts diminish rather than amplify a bold color choice.
- Structured feedback framing beats vague preference-testing: replace "do you like it?" with specific questions tied to brand adjectives ("does this feel [adjective]?", "memorable after 5 seconds?", "distinct from competitors you know?") and look for patterns across 5-10 target-audience reviewers, not single opinions.

## Patterns (name — when to use — when to avoid)
- Low-desaturation/light shade + white — wellness, personal care, calming positioning — avoid for brands needing to project authority/urgency.
- High-saturation/bright shade — tech, gaming, digital-first, youth-oriented — avoid for brands needing conservative/traditional trust signals.
- Very-dark/desaturated shade + gold or black — premium, financial-advisory, luxury — avoid for accessible/mass-market positioning where it will read as exclusionary.

## Anti-patterns
- Selecting a shade without checking it against black-and-white / single-color reproduction.
- Pairing a saturated base color with multiple other bright colors ("visual chaos").
- Skipping real-world testing (email signature, business card, dark and light backgrounds) before finalizing.

## Checklist (only if supported)
- Design confirmed to work in full black-and-white.
- Tested at minimum sizes (~0.5in print, 16px digital) on light and dark backgrounds.
- Full variant set produced: full color, black, white/reversed, single-color.
- Feedback gathered via specific brand-adjective questions from 5-10 target-audience members, looking for patterns not single opinions.
