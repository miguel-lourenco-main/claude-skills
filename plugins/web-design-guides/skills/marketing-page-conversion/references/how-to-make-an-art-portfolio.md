## Use when
Designing or auditing any hiring-facing creative portfolio (design, illustration, art, game art) where you need to survive a reviewer's actual triage process, not just look good.

## Rules
- Reviews pass through three sequential filters, each with a different job: technical presentation (0-10s: layout, clarity of specialization, working links, mobile) → strategic curation (10-180s: does project selection match what they're hiring for) → documented problem-solving (3-10+ min: process, iteration, judgment). Design for all three explicitly, in that order.
- In filter one, unclear specialization is a silent killer: if a reviewer can't tell in seconds whether you're an illustrator, UI designer, or 3D artist, they move on. Lead with a stated specialization.
- Process documentation should start during the project, not be reconstructed after: capture screenshots at key decision points (early concepts, alternatives considered, pivotal iterations) and short rationale notes as you go.
- Every project should answer five questions: What was the challenge? What did you do? How did you approach it? Why these choices? What was the impact?
- Curate by role/audience, not chronology: organize the portfolio by capability ("Character Design," "UI/UX Projects") because that's how hiring managers search, not by year.
- Piece counts by context: 3-5 top projects for a scannable general job-application portfolio (two-minute review target); 5-7 complete case studies for recent grads; 20-25 pages for entertainment/animation-specific reviews; three full campaigns for creative-direction roles. Match the count to the review convention of the field.
- Maintain multiple tailored portfolio versions rather than one universal one when applying across different role types.
- Balance client work (proves you can deliver under constraints) with personal projects (proves vision and self-direction); use personal work specifically to fill capability gaps the client work doesn't demonstrate.
- Front-load your strongest work — the first pieces get the attention budget from filter one.
- Prefer a visible, clickable direct email over a contact-form-only setup; most reviewers are frustrated when they can't find one. Forms are fine as a spam-filtering supplement, never a replacement.
- Frame the About page around outcomes, not responsibilities: "Designed 47 character models across 3 shipped titles including a protagonist with 2M+ downloads" beats "Responsible for character design."
- Mobile-first is non-negotiable: ~60% of portfolio reviews happen on mobile. 44x44px touch targets, fluid grid/flex layouts, correct viewport meta tag, images <500KB each with ~1-1.5MB total page weight target.
- Project descriptions of 200-300+ words serve both SEO and reviewer context — thin captions under-explain the work.

## Patterns (name — when to use — when to avoid)
- Capability-based navigation ("Character Design," "Editorial Illustration") — whenever a reviewer is searching for a specific skill match — avoid only for identity-first portfolios where the person, not a category, is the pitch.
- Consistent quality across all shown work — always — including a weaker piece "for variety" is a rejection risk, not a feature.

## Anti-patterns
- Chronological-only organization that forces reviewers to hunt for relevant work.
- Reconstructing "process" documentation after the fact from memory instead of capturing it live.
- Contact-form-only with no visible email.
- Mixing skill levels/quality tiers in one undifferentiated grid.

## Checklist (only if supported)
- Specialization is stated and legible within the first 10 seconds.
- Piece count matches field convention (3-5 general / 5-7 grad / 20-25 entertainment / 3 campaigns creative-direction).
- Every project answers challenge/action/approach/rationale/impact.
- Portfolio organized by capability, strongest work first.
- Direct email visible; mobile tested for load time and touch targets.
