## Use when
Designing a manufacturing, industrial-supplier, or technical-B2B site where the buyer is an engineer or procurement professional doing self-service research before ever contacting sales.

## Rules
- Manufacturing site conversion baselines are low: 2.2% average vs 2.9% general B2B average, with 2-5% achievable (per First Page Sage research, per guide) — judge a redesign against these, not consumer ecommerce benchmarks.
- Industrial buyers scan for data points, they don't read linearly — typography and hierarchy exist to accelerate scanning speed, not to look polished. Limit to one sans-serif for UI/body (+ optional serif for editorial headlines); keep body line length in the 50-75 character range for technical content (unsourced guideline in guide).
- Use real operational footage over stock photography — procurement/engineering buyers need to see actual capability before engaging. Desktop-first video, since industrial buyers research during work hours on desktop.
- Certifications (ISO 9001, AS9100, etc.) are purchase-order-acceptance prerequisites in some industries, not soft trust signals — missing the required certification can eliminate a supplier before any site interaction happens. Match the certification shown to the buyer's industry (e.g., AS9100 specifically for aerospace, not just ISO 9001) and include certificate numbers/dates/registry links for independent verification.
- Buyers do most of their research before contacting sales — cited at ~70% of the buying journey completed pre-contact (per 6sense research, per guide) — so self-service content (certs, specs, case studies) has to carry the persuasion work alone.
- Quote/RFQ forms: keep to 3-5 fields for standard B2B, 4-6 for complex/technical qualification; forms beyond 7 fields see much higher abandonment (unsourced in guide). Minimize dropdowns — they can reduce conversion versus simpler input types (per Unbounce research, per guide). Fast follow-up response outperforms delayed response.
- For manufacturers selling into multiple verticals, build dedicated industry-specific landing pages (e.g., aerospace, automotive, medical) under one shared design system/nav, rather than one generic page trying to speak to every buyer.
- Product catalogs need to support self-service technical lookup: part-number search, filterable specs, comparison tools, downloadable CAD files — buyers are trying to avoid contacting sales during research.
- Case studies need measurable outcomes (ROI %, efficiency gains, operational KPIs) structured as problem-solution-result; generic testimonials underperform because buyers rely more heavily on content-driven proof than in prior years (67% cited, per Demand Gen Report 2020 Content Preferences Study, per guide).
- Prioritize desktop for detailed research, but keep mobile functional for field buyers: quote requests, contact, and dealer/location lookup must work on a phone even if desktop is the primary research surface.
- Interactive configurators self-qualify leads on both budget (real-time pricing) and technical fit (constraint-based logic) before a human is involved. CPQ-equipped companies see 17% higher lead conversion (unsourced in guide); configurators can cut time-to-quote 60-85% (per PROS performance data, per guide).
- Load-time sensitivity is high for heavy visual/spec-laden pages: target ≤2.4s. In one performance test, 2.4s pages converted at 1.9%, 3.3s pages at 1.5%, and pages over 4.2s fell below 1% (per mPulse Mobile testing, per guide); every 100ms of added delay can cost up to 7% conversion (per Akamai research, per guide).
- Lead with outcomes in plain language over technical jargon in value propositions — "reduce machining time by 40%" over "advanced high-speed milling capabilities."
- Segment social proof/logos by industry vertical rather than showing one undifferentiated logo wall; the number of brands considered per B2B buying journey is rising (up 62% since 2021, per dentsu Superpowers Index research, per guide), so undifferentiated proof carries less weight than it used to.

## Patterns (name — when to use — when to avoid)
- **Certification-as-gate, not badge** — surface the industry-specific required certification prominently in header/hero with verifiable numbers. Use whenever the vertical has regulatory/procurement-mandated certifications; skip the heavy treatment for buyers where certification is merely reassuring rather than a hard requirement.
- **Vertical landing pages under one system** — dedicated pages per served industry, shared nav/design. Use when serving 3+ distinct verticals with different pain points; avoid when the product line genuinely serves one buyer type (adds maintenance for no segmentation benefit).
- **Self-qualifying configurator** — real-time pricing + constraint logic before human contact. Use for configurable/complex products with meaningful price variation; avoid for simple catalog items where a spec sheet already answers fit.
- **Minimal-field RFQ form** — 3-6 fields, no unnecessary dropdowns. Use for all industrial quote/contact forms; only add fields the sales team will actually act on immediately.

## Anti-patterns
- Displaying only a generic certification (e.g., ISO 9001) to a buyer segment that requires a specific one (e.g., AS9100) — reads as under-qualified.
- RFQ forms with more than ~6-7 fields or heavy dropdown use.
- Generic testimonials without named metrics.
- Treating mobile as equal priority to desktop for this buyer (it's supportive, not primary).

## Checklist
- [ ] Correct industry-specific certification displayed with verifiable numbers, not just a generic badge
- [ ] Quote/RFQ form has ≤6 fields, minimal dropdowns
- [ ] Product catalog supports part-number search and spec filtering
- [ ] Case studies show named metrics, structured problem→solution→result
- [ ] Page load ≤2.4s on spec/product pages
- [ ] Social proof segmented by vertical
- [ ] Mobile supports quote/contact/dealer-locator even if desktop is primary
