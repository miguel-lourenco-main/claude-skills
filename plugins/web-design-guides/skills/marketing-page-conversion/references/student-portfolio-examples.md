## Use when
Building a first professional portfolio with limited paid experience (students, career switchers, early-career), or reviewing one for common credibility traps.

## Rules
- The three traits shared by portfolios that get hires: they show process (not just polished results), they lead with the strongest work first, and they respect the reviewer's time (fast to scan, clear path to relevant content).
- Curating 3-5 strong projects matters more than the platform used to build the site — platform choice is a distant second-order decision.
- Separate distinct content types into their own navigation branches when a field expects it (e.g. "Projects" vs. "Research" for engineering) — this gives reviewers a direct path to the section relevant to them instead of forcing a linear read.
- A custom-built site differentiates when your peer cohort is clustered on the same template platform (Squarespace/Canva) — the differentiation value depends entirely on what your specific competitive set is using.
- If you break visual/structural convention (unconventional layout, playful storytelling), your execution quality must justify it — the fallback is a clean, minimal layout that undersells nothing.
- Research-heavy or academic portfolios should still translate work for non-specialist screeners, not just domain experts.
- An embedded AI chatbot trained on your own case studies can function as an interactive resume, but the accuracy bar is high: wrong answers about your own experience damage trust faster than a typo would, and it requires well-organized source content written before the assistant, not after.

## Patterns (name — when to use — when to avoid)
- Identity-first homepage framing (e.g. "I am a writer") — when you're confident in a single direction — avoid when you're deliberately exploring multiple career paths; use project-based framing instead for flexibility.
- Interactive/experimental storytelling (visual, blog-style write-ups, unconventional navigation) — when your execution skill can carry the risk — avoid when visual polish doesn't yet match the creative ambition.
- University-hosted platform (e.g. WordPress via a school program) — fine short-term for students — avoid as a permanent home: these sites disappear after graduation, so migrate content to an owned domain before losing access.

## Anti-patterns
- Fabricated or invented testimonials (use quantifiable outcomes instead if you lack real quotes).
- Detailed case studies that become walls of unbroken text — break them up with visual artifacts (sketches, forms, prototypes) at each stage.
- Treating "never finished" as an acceptable state — a shipped, imperfect portfolio beats a perfect unfinished one.

## Checklist (only if supported)
- 3-5 strongest projects selected, front-loaded.
- Each project shows process artifacts, not only final output.
- Navigation separates content types a reviewer would search independently.
- Testimonials/claims are all real and verifiable.
- Site lives on an owned domain, not a platform you'll lose access to.
